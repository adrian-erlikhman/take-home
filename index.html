For patients and the people caring for them

Leave the hospital with a plan, not a pile of papers.

Paste your discharge instructions. Take Home turns them into a daily medicine schedule, the warning signs to watch for, your appointments with real dates, and the questions to ask your pharmacist. Every line points back to your papers.

Runs in your browser. Nothing you paste leaves this page unless you turn on Claude with your own key. Take Home doesn't give medical advice: it rearranges what your care team wrote, and asks questions about it.

Your discharge papers

Paste the text of your discharge instructions or after-visit summary, or load the PDF. Keep the headings (like “Medications” and “Call your doctor if”). That's how Take Home finds each part.

Use Claude (optional, your own API key)

Claude can type up a photo of printed papers (you check it against the paper before making a plan), and sort the lines of a letter with an unusual layout. When sorting, every line it returns has to appear in your letter, numbers included, or it's dropped. Claude can also answer questions about your papers, but only by quoting them, and translate your plan. If the translation changes, adds or drops a single number, it's thrown out. Your key stays in this tab and goes only to api.anthropic.com.

How it works

Read

Rules, not guesses. Take Home finds the sections of your papers by their headings, then reads each medicine line for its name, strength, how many, how often, for how long, and whether it's new, changed or stopped.

Arrange

Each medicine goes on a daily schedule built around when you wake up and go to bed. Appointments get real dates from your discharge date. Warning signs are split into “call 911” and “call your doctor.”

Ask

It never changes a dose. When something needs a second look, like two medicines that both contain acetaminophen, or “as needed” with no daily limit, it writes the question for your pharmacist.

Take Home is open source (MIT). Built by Adrian Erlikhman and Michael Tarekegn. The sample patients, hospitals and phone numbers are made up. This is not medical advice: follow your care team's instructions, and call 911 in an emergency.
